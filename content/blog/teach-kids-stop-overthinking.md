Your 8-year-old staring at two t-shirts for five minutes. It's a shirt. But for them, it feels like the biggest decision of their life.

Sound familiar? You're not alone. Many kids get stuck on tiny decisions, turning what should take seconds into what feels like a high-stakes moment. The good news: this is fixable with some simple tools and language.

If the overthinking looks more like distress than deliberation, psychologist Eileen Kennedy-Moore's guide to [when your child can't decide](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/growing-friendships/201906/when-your-child-can-t-decide) offers gentle scripts for those moments.

## Why Kids Overthink (It's Not What You Think)

Adults often assume kids overthink because they're being dramatic, seeking attention, or testing boundaries. Sometimes that's true. But most of the time, 's happening:

### They're Still Learning How Decisions Work

For adults, decision-making is automatic. We know that picking a shirt doesn't matter in the grand scheme. Kids haven't built that mental framework yet. To them, every choice can feel important.

### They Feel Pressure to Get It "Right"

 Kids often believe there is a perfect choice and a wrong choice. For most decisions, any option works fine. That gap in understanding creates stress around choices adults find trivial.


### They're Mirroring Your Behavior

If adults in the house struggle with decisions or talk about "what if" scenarios, kids pick up on that energy. Overthinking becomes a habit, not a kid problem.

## The Simple Framework to Teach Them

 Teach your kids this sentence. Repeat it together until it becomes automatic:


 "Most decisions are small. Pick one and move on."


 Sounds too simple? It works. The goal isn't to make every decision feel meaningless. It's to help kids build a mental radar that says: "This is not a big deal decision. I can decide fast."


## The "Good Enough" Rule

Teach kids about the concept of "good enough." Not every choice needs to be the perfect choice. A shirt that's not their absolute favorite still keeps them warm. A lunch that's not their top pick still fills their . A show that's not their favorite is still entertainment.

 Perfect is the enemy of done. This is a lesson that serves kids their whole lives. Starting with small decisions makes it easier to grasp.


## Use Tools to Remove the Pressure

When kids struggle with making a choice, a tool can help. because tools remove the responsibility of being "right."

 Try these:


- **Coin flips:**For two options, flip. Heads one, tails the other. Whatever lands is the choice. No stress, no second-guessing.

- **Spin wheels:** Put options on a wheel, spin, and done. Great for multiple choices like "which game to play" or "what order to do things in."

- **Yes/no tools:**For simple either/or decisions, sometimes you need a clear answer. A quick digital coin or random picker removes all the drama.

## The Language That Helps

Sometimes what helps kids isn't a tool , it's what you say. Try these phrases:

### "Pick one. You can change next time."

 This removes permanence. They know it's not forever, so the stakes feel lower.


### "There is no wrong choice here."

 Say this when you see them getting stuck. It's permission to stop analyzing.


### "What's the worst that could happen?"

the answer is "nothing." Sometimes kids need to hear themselves say it.

### "Thirty seconds. Let's decide and move on."

 Give them a time limit. Challenge them. Make it a game. Most of the time, they can decide faster than they think.


## When Overthinking Signals Something Bigger

 Sometimes kids overthink because there's genuine [anxiety](https://childmind.org/article/what-to-do-and-not-do-when-children-are-anxious/) underneath. If your child:


- Freezes on decisions that should be easy

- Can't sleep because they're replaying choices they made

- asks for reassurance after deciding

- Gets upset (crying, stomachaches, panic) over choices

 That's a signal to dig deeper. It might be anxiety, perfectionism, or something else worth addressing with more support. The tools and phrases above can still help, but they might need more context and patience.


## Practice Makes Progress

 Don't expect overnight change. Teaching kids to make faster decisions is a skill that develops over time. Start with low-stakes decisions (which cup, which snack, which toy) and work up from there.


Celebrate when they decide . Point it out: "You picked your outfit fast today! That showed good decision-making." Positive feedback builds the habit.

 And remember: you're teaching them a life skill. Years from now, they won't remember which shirt they picked today. But they'll remember that they learned to make decisions without getting stuck. That's the real lesson.

> **Try this tonight:** At dinner, have everyone — parents included — share "one decision I made fast today and how it turned out." Most fast decisions turn out fine, and hearing that pattern out loud, night after night, is the quiet cure for overthinking.

## FAQ: Overthinking, Underdeciding

### Is overthinking ever a good sign?

Often, yes — it usually lives in bright, imaginative kids who can see many outcomes at once. The skill they're missing isn't intelligence, it's *calibration*: matching the amount of thinking to the size of the choice. You're not trying to make them think less everywhere — just less about socks.

### What's a realistic goal — no overthinking at all?

No. The goal is a working "good enough" switch: big, rare decisions get real deliberation; daily decisions get ten seconds and a shrug. When your child can say "this one doesn't need my whole brain," the job is done — even if they still agonize over their birthday party theme.

### My child overthinks because a past choice went badly. How do I reset that?

Name the story, then shrink it: "The sleepover was a rough night. That was one choice out of hundreds — most of your picks go fine." Then stack small wins: a week of tiny, fast, successful choices rebuilds the base rate their brain is drawing from. Letting kids [learn from imperfect outcomes](https://childmind.org/article/how-to-help-kids-learn-to-fail/) is what dissolves the flinch — avoiding all risk preserves it.

### Should I stop them mid-spiral and just decide?

Interrupt the spiral, but hand back the wheel: "Too much thinking for a small choice — pick the one your gut said first." The first-instinct pick is usually the real preference, and practicing that retrieval is the actual skill.
