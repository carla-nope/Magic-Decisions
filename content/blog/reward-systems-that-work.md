Navigating the world of parenting often involves finding effective ways to encourage positive behavior in children. Reward systems can be powerful tools, but their effectiveness hinges on understanding the psychology behind them. This guide explores how to implement reward systems that genuinely foster growth and [motivation](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/theory/), rather than inadvertently undermining it.


## The Double-Edged Sword of Rewards: Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation


At the heart of effective reward systems lies a crucial distinction: **intrinsic versus extrinsic motivation**. Intrinsic motivation comes from within—the joy of learning, the satisfaction of accomplishment, or the inherent interest in an activity. Extrinsic motivation, conversely, is driven by external factors, such as praise, money, or prizes. While extrinsic rewards can be effective for short-term behavioral changes, they can sometimes have unintended consequences, particularly for tasks children already enjoy.


This phenomenon is known as the **overjustification effect**, extensively researched by psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan. Their work suggests that when an external reward is introduced for an activity that was previously intrinsically motivating, the child may begin to attribute their engagement to the reward rather than their own enjoyment. Once the reward is removed, their interest in the activity can diminish significantly. This doesn't mean all rewards are bad, but it highlights the importance of thoughtful application.


>
 "The central tenet of self-determination theory, developed by Deci and Ryan, is that people are motivated to grow and change by three innate psychological needs: competence, relatedness, and autonomy."


Understanding this balance is key to designing systems that support a child's natural drive to learn and achieve. For instance, if your child loves reading, offering a prize for every book they finish might inadvertently turn reading into a chore for a reward, rather than a joyful pursuit. Consider how you can [motivate kids without nagging](/blog/motivate-kids-without-nagging), focusing on their internal drive.


## The Four Types of Rewards: A Strategic Overview


Not all rewards are created equal. Recognizing the different types helps in choosing the most appropriate one for a given situation:


 - **Tangible Rewards:** These are physical items like toys, stickers, or treats. They are often easy to implement and can be highly motivating for young children. However, they carry the highest risk of the overjustification effect if not used carefully.

 - **Activity Rewards:** These involve granting access to preferred activities, such as extra screen time, a trip to the park, or choosing dinner. They can be very effective as they often align with a child's interests and can feel less like a bribe than tangible items.

 - **Social Rewards:** Praise, hugs, high-fives, and quality time are powerful social rewards. They reinforce positive behavior by strengthening the parent-child bond and building self-esteem. These are generally the safest and most effective for fostering intrinsic motivation.

 - **Token Economy:** This system uses tokens (e.g., stars, points, marbles) that can be exchanged for other rewards. It teaches delayed gratification and can be customized to offer a variety of tangible or activity rewards. This is often seen in [gamified chore systems for families](/blog/gamified-chore-systems-families).


#### 💡 Tip for Success


When using tangible or activity rewards, pair them with social rewards. For example, when giving a sticker, also offer specific praise like, "I noticed how hard you worked on your homework; that sticker shows your effort!"


## When to Reward and When to Reconsider


The "what" of rewarding is as important as the "how." Certain tasks are more suitable for reward systems than others:


 - **Safe to Reward:** Tasks that are not inherently enjoyable, such as chores, homework completion (especially difficult subjects), or developing new, challenging skills. These are areas where extrinsic motivation can kickstart behavior until intrinsic satisfaction or mastery takes over. Think about how you can [turn chores into a game](/blog/turn-chores-into-a-game) to make them more engaging.

 - **Proceed with Caution:** Activities that children already find pleasurable, like reading, creative play, or helping others. For these, focus on social rewards and opportunities for autonomy rather than tangible incentives.

 - **Red Flags:** Never reward for basic expectations like kindness, honesty, or emotional regulation. These are fundamental values that should be taught through modeling, discussion, and natural consequences, not external bribes.


## Designing a Reward System That Fades Gracefully


The ultimate goal of any reward system is to become unnecessary. An effective system is designed to fade out over time, transferring control from external motivators to internal ones. The transition works like this:


 - **Start with Clear Expectations:** Define the specific behaviors you want to encourage. Make sure your child understands what they need to do to earn a reward.

 - **Choose Appropriate Rewards:** Begin with rewards that are motivating but gradually shift towards more intrinsic forms. For instance, move from small toys to activity time, then to praise and recognition.

 - **Increase the Interval:** Initially, reward frequently for small steps. As the behavior becomes more consistent, gradually increase the time or effort required to earn a reward. Instead of daily, move to weekly, then monthly.

 - **Emphasize Effort and Progress:** Always highlight the child's effort, perseverance, and the positive feelings associated with their achievements. "You must feel so proud of finishing your project!" is more impactful than just "Good job."

 - **Involve the Child:** Allow children to have a say in the types of rewards and the goals. This fosters a sense of autonomy and ownership, aligning with self-determination theory.


## Red Flags: When a Reward System Backfires


Even with the best intentions, reward systems can go awry. Watch out for these signs that your system might be doing more harm than good:


 - **"No Reward, No Work":** If your child refuses to perform a task without an explicit reward, the system has likely undermined their intrinsic motivation.

 - **Diminished Quality:** Children might rush through tasks just to get the reward, sacrificing quality for speed.

 - **Cheating or Manipulation:** If children are finding ways to game the system or negotiate for higher rewards, it's a sign that the focus has shifted from the behavior to the prize.

 - **Escalating Demands:** You find yourself needing to offer increasingly larger or more frequent rewards to maintain the same level of effort.

 - **Negative Emotional Responses:** Frustration, anger, or resentment when a reward is not earned or is perceived as unfair.


 🧠

Research by Alfie Kohn suggests that rewards often fail to produce lasting change and can even reduce interest in activities.


If you observe these red flags, it's time to re-evaluate and adjust your approach. Sometimes, removing the reward system and focusing on natural consequences, logical reasoning, and fostering a sense of responsibility can be more effective.


## Conclusion


Reward systems, when used thoughtfully and strategically, can be valuable tools in a parent's toolkit. By understanding the nuances of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, choosing appropriate rewards, and designing systems that encourage gradual [independence](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Growing-Independence-Tips-for-Parents-of-Young-Children.aspx), you can help your children develop lasting positive behaviors and a genuine love for learning and accomplishment. The goal isn't just to get them to do something, but to help them want to do it.
